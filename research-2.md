# When citizens set the budget and experts spend it: formal models and empirical failures

Two-group resource allocation problems — where a less-trusted group's assessed welfare determines a resource envelope and a more-trusted expert group optimises within it — have no unified formal treatment in the literature, but rich theoretical fragments and devastating empirical evidence exist across insurance, welfare, and climate adaptation finance. **The central finding is that the lexicographic priority (citizens first, experts second) is almost universally violated in practice**, through two structural failure modes: expert capture of the aggregation rule g(·) and decoupling of actual flows from formally stated needs-based principles. The theoretical machinery to model this structure exists in bilevel optimisation, claims-problem axiomatics, and mechanism design with limited commitment, but these traditions have not been synthesised into a unified framework. In climate adaptation finance — the primary domain of interest — the evidence is especially stark: **international public adaptation finance shows no statistically significant correlation with vulnerability for small island developing states**, and the global adaptation financing gap stands at 12–14 times current flows.

## 1. Formal theoretical models: four traditions that almost but never fully capture the structure

### The claims-problem axiomatics provide the most direct formalisation of g(·)

The citizen-side aggregation rule g(·) finds its clearest formalisation in the bankruptcy/claims literature originating with O'Neill (1982, *Mathematical Social Sciences*) and systematised by Thomson (2003, 2015, *Mathematical Social Sciences*; 2019, *How to Divide When There Isn't Enough*, Cambridge University Press). In this framework, agents hold claims c_i on a scarce resource E where Σc_i > E, and a rule r(c, E) → x allocates E subject to 0 ≤ x_i ≤ c_i and Σx_i = E. Three canonical rules are axiomatically characterised: the **proportional rule** (x_i = λc_i, characterised by composition and consistency), **constrained equal awards** (x_i = min(c_i, λ), egalitarian, prioritising smaller claims), and **constrained equal losses** (x_i = max(0, c_i − λ), equal sacrifice). Young (1987, *Mathematics of Operations Research*) showed each parametric rule minimises a social cost function, providing the optimisation-theoretic foundation. Xue (2018, *Social Choice and Welfare*) extended the framework to stochastic needs, introducing "equal-quantile rules" for allocation under uncertainty about satiation points.

These rules are precisely the g(·) functions mapping citizen claims to resource allocations. What the claims literature lacks is the second tier: the expert optimisation problem within X(R). This gap is filled by bilevel programming, where an upper level (welfare aggregation determining R) constrains a lower level (expert optimisation). The mathematical structure — formalised in Dempe (2002, *Foundations of Bilevel Programming*, Springer) and Colson, Marcotte & Savard (2007, *Annals of Operations Research*) — inherently enforces the lexicographic priority: the upper level cannot be overridden by the lower level. Lexicographic optimisation theory itself was axiomatised by Hammond (1976, *Econometrica*), who showed that anonymity, strong Pareto, and an equity condition uniquely characterise the leximin ordering — the formal analogue of maximising the worst-off group's welfare before considering efficiency.

### Capabilities formalisations and community targeting provide the normative and empirical substance

Sen (1985, *Commodities and Capabilities*, North-Holland) provided the most formal elaboration of the capability approach: functionings b_i = f_i(c(x_i)) where x_i is a commodity vector, c(·) a characteristics function, and f_i(·) a personal utilisation function. The capability set Q_i = {b_i | b_i = f_i(c(x_i)), x_i ∈ X_i} is heterogeneous across agents, providing the normative foundation for why g(·) must be needs-sensitive rather than resource-egalitarian. Dagsvik (2013, *Theory and Decision*) made the capability set operational via random scale models. Fleurbaey and Maniquet (2011, *A Theory of Fairness and Social Welfare*, Cambridge; 2018, *Journal of Economic Literature*) provided the axiomatic foundations for determining which dimensions of heterogeneity should enter g(·) — compensating for circumstances beyond agents' control while respecting responsibility for preferences. Their "equivalent income" concept offers a **needs-adjusted welfare metric** that resolves the aggregation problem when citizens differ in both needs and preferences.

Community-based targeting models directly implement the two-group structure empirically. Galasso and Ravallion (2005, *Journal of Public Economics*) formalised a two-tier system where a centre allocates G_i to village i, and within-village allocation follows Nash bargaining with heterogeneous weights: W = Σ_k β_k U_k(y_k + t_k) subject to Σt_k = G_i/H_i. Alatas, Banerjee, Hanna, Olken and Tobias (2012, *American Economic Review*) demonstrated experimentally that community-based targeting applies a different welfare concept than expert-designed proxy means tests — emphasising vulnerability and earning potential over consumption poverty — and generates substantially higher legitimacy. Hussam, Rigol and Roth (2022, *American Economic Review*) showed that community knowledge can be elicited through simple mechanisms, but strategic distortion arises when informants can influence distribution, creating a standard mechanism design problem.

### Mechanism design with limited commitment captures the expert defection problem

Doval and Skreta (2022, *Econometrica*) developed the foundational theory for settings where a designer cannot commit to future mechanisms. Their revelation principle analogue for dynamic mechanism-selection games shows that **canonical mechanisms encode both allocation rules and disclosure rules** — the amount the designer learns about agents' types is itself a design variable. This directly applies to the citizen-expert model: the expert cannot commit not to revise allocations once citizen welfare information is revealed. Sun (2025, working paper, UCL/CEMFI) established the most directly relevant impossibility result for welfare-constrained policy design: **no statistical rule can simultaneously achieve uniform welfare efficiency and budget feasibility** over a wide class of data-generating processes when potential costs are unknown.

## 2. Insurance: where actuarial accuracy paradoxically undermines needs-based coverage

### Formal models embed the screening problem at the heart of g(·)

Insurance economics provides some of the most precise formalisations of the needs-to-coverage mapping. Chetty's (2006, *Journal of Public Economics*) sufficient-statistics framework derives the optimal benefit level b* = f(Δc/c, ε, γ) — a function of consumption drop upon adverse event, behavioural elasticity, and risk aversion — precisely specifying how aggregate need maps to coverage. Diamond and Sheshinski (1995, *Journal of Public Economics*) formalised the disability screening problem where an imperfect test mediates between true need and benefit receipt, generating Type I and Type II errors that directly depend on expert (examiner) operation of the screening device.

Most directly relevant to the two-group structure, Bhaskar, McClellan and Sadler (2023, *American Economic Review*) modelled insurance regulation as a delegation problem: a regulator restricts the menus of contracts an informed firm may offer, where the regulatory constraint on the menu **is** the g(·) function. They showed that when consumer types and firm signals are ordered by coverage need, the regulator can leverage firm information by forcing additional options on each menu — but the firm's private signal may be used self-interestedly unless constraints are well-designed. Lindholm, Richman, Tsanakas and Wüthrich (2022, *ASTIN Bulletin*) formalised discrimination-free insurance pricing using a causal framework, where actuarial optimisation is explicitly constrained by a fairness requirement that removes proxy discrimination flowing through protected attributes.

### Expert capture operates through structural conflicts, selection bias, and algorithmic amplification

The **Unum/Provident scandal** (2004–2008) provides the most extensively documented case of expert capture in insurance. Regulators from 48 US states found systematic denial of claims based on fabricated evidentiary requirements, cherry-picking of medical reviewers (13 of 13 denials in one reviewer's record), and tying adjuster compensation to profitability. Over **200,000 denied claims were reopened, with more than 45% reversed**, yielding $558 million in additional benefits. The US Supreme Court in *Metropolitan Life v. Glenn* (554 U.S. 105, 2008) formally recognised that when an insurer both evaluates and pays claims, a structural conflict of interest exists — judicial acknowledgement that the expert operating g(·) is simultaneously the entity benefiting from denying needs.

In health insurance, prior authorisation operates as an expert gatekeeping mechanism generating systematic wedges between clinical need and coverage. **AMA surveys report 93% of physicians observe care delays from prior authorisation**, with 29% reporting serious adverse events. Medicare Advantage denies **17% of initial claims, with 57% of denials ultimately overturned** — direct evidence that initial expert assessments systematically understate need.

### Decoupling manifests as an actuarial accuracy paradox

The most structurally revealing finding is that **more accurate actuarial pricing can undermine needs-based coverage**. FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0 reform, designed to make flood insurance premiums actuarially fair, caused up to **39% decline in new policies** in highest-premium areas, with lower-income communities disproportionately dropping coverage (Gourevitch, Snyder & Kousky, 2025). NFIP premiums are regressive as a share of coverage (Shabman & Kousky, 2017, *B.E. Journal of Economic Analysis & Policy*), and rural communities cross-subsidise urban Community Rating System discounts by hundreds of dollars because administrative capacity — not flood risk — determines who benefits (2025, *One Earth*). This is a fundamental tension: the expert's objective function (actuarial accuracy) directly conflicts with the citizen-determined norm (coverage proportional to need).

## 3. Welfare state: the screening–complexity–take-up chain produces systematic exclusion

### Optimal taxation theory formalises g(·) through sufficient statistics

The Mirrlees (1971, *Review of Economic Studies*) framework and its modern successors (Saez, 2002, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*; Piketty & Saez, 2013, *Handbook of Public Economics*) formalise g(·) as the optimal tax-transfer schedule T(z), where social marginal welfare weights and behavioural elasticities determine its shape. Akerlof's (1978, *American Economic Review*) tagging model extends g(·) to multiple observable characteristics, formalising categorical benefit rules. Parsons (1996, *Journal of Public Economics*) showed how imperfect tagging generates two-sided classification errors analogous to the insurance screening problem.

The most complete formal model linking expert and citizen layers is **Kleven and Kopczuk (2011, *American Economic Journal: Economic Policy*)**, who modelled programme complexity as a by-product of screening. Experts choose screening intensity α, which improves targeting accuracy but increases applicant compliance costs f(α), inducing incomplete take-up. The actual benefit allocation becomes g(·) × Pr(acceptance|α) × Pr(take-up|f(α)) — precisely showing how expert decisions about screening create decoupling. Their key finding: **optimal programmes feature high complexity, incomplete take-up, and classification errors of both types**. Boadway, Marceau and Sato (1999, *Journal of Public Economics*) directly modelled the principal-agent problem between welfare system designers and caseworkers who may shirk, endogenously generating the needs-to-benefit gap.

### Bureaucratic bias is formally predicted and empirically confirmed

Prendergast (2007, *American Economic Review*) established three results with profound implications: bureaucrats should be biased (pro- or anti-client depending on oversight structure), complaint-driven oversight may make anti-client bias optimal, and self-selection into bureaucracies is bifurcated. Slough (2024, working paper, Columbia) extended this to show that citizens with lower complaint costs receive systematically better service — an audit experiment in Colombia's SISBÉN proxy means testing system confirmed that **bureaucratic bias correlates with municipal-level underprovision**.

The creaming and parking literature provides extensive evidence of capture in welfare-to-work programmes. Carter and Whitworth (2015, *Journal of Social Policy*) found the UK Work Programme's differential payment groups offer **virtually no explanatory power** for employment outcomes — most variation is within, not between groups, meaning the programme designs in creaming rather than preventing it. Koning and Heinrich (2013, *Journal of Policy Analysis and Management*) found that fully performance-contingent contracts in Dutch welfare services increased placements only for more readily employable workers. Brodkin and Majmundar (2010, *Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory*) documented how organisations operate as informal gatekeepers through "hidden costs" — complicated procedures that produce administrative exclusion of eligible individuals.

### Non-take-up creates massive decoupling between formal entitlement and actual receipt

Herd and Moynihan (2018, *Administrative Burden*, Russell Sage; 2025, *Journal of Economic Perspectives*) established that administrative burdens — learning costs, compliance costs, and psychological costs — are not accidental but **deliberate policy instruments** ("policymaking by other means") that systematically limit access for those with fewest resources and greatest needs. Ko and Moffitt (2022, NBER WP 30148) documented substantial non-take-up across programmes: SNAP take-up approximately **80%**, EITC 78–80%, Medicaid varying widely. In China's Dibao programme, approximately 90% of eligibles do not receive benefits while approximately 90% of recipients are estimated ineligible. Moffitt's (1983, *American Economic Review*) formal model showed welfare stigma endogenously creates wedges between formal eligibility and participation.

At the global level, the evidence is equally damning. Margitic and Ravallion (2019, *Journal of Development Economics*) found that social assistance in developing countries **lifts the consumption floor by only 1.5 cents per day on average** — less than 10% of mean spending. Cornia and Stewart's (1995) framework of dual targeting errors (Type I exclusion, Type II leakage) remains the standard lens, while Coady, Grosh and Hoddinott (2004, World Bank) found the median targeted programme transfers only 25% more to the poor than a universal allocation — better than random but far from the theoretical optimum.

## 4. Climate adaptation finance: the starkest evidence of structural decoupling

### Vulnerability indices as g(·) functions vary enormously by construction

Climate adaptation finance allocation relies on composite vulnerability indices whose mathematical properties directly determine who receives resources. The **ND-GAIN Country Index** (Chen et al., 2015, Notre Dame) uses hierarchical equal-weight arithmetic means: V = (1/6)Σ V_s across six sectors, each decomposed as V_s = (1/3)(Exposure + Sensitivity + (1 − AdaptiveCapacity)), with indicators normalised to [0,1] using fixed goalposts. This additive structure is fully compensatory — high adaptive capacity in one sector offsets extreme vulnerability in another.

The **Physical Vulnerability to Climate Change Index** (Feindouno, Guillaumont & Simonet, 2020, *Ecological Economics*) deliberately uses quadratic mean (root-mean-square) aggregation with country-specific exposure weights, making it non-compensatory and restricted to exogenous physical vulnerability — countries cannot manipulate scores through policy choices. Zanella, Camanho and Dias (2020, *Ecological Indicators*) applied Data Envelopment Analysis to generate endogenous, country-specific weights via linear programming, revealing which vulnerability dimensions are most binding per country. Kim et al. (2019, *Scientific Reports*) compared six aggregation frameworks and found **large ranking fluctuations and reversals across methods**, recommending multiplicative composites that prevent full compensation between low and high components.

The institutional allocation rules of major climate funds do not use formula-based allocation. The **Green Climate Fund** operates a competitive proposal process with portfolio-level floor constraints (≥50% of adaptation funding to LDCs, SIDS, and African states) and six investment criteria evaluated by an Independent Technical Advisory Panel. The **Adaptation Fund** uses qualitative vulnerability criteria without a quantitative formula — vulnerability remains, in Persson and Remling's (2014) words, "politically and scientifically ambiguous." Barr, Fankhauser and Hamilton (2010, *Mitigation and Adaptation Strategies for Global Change*) proposed an IDA-style performance-based allocation formula: A_i ∝ (Vulnerability_i)^α × (ImplementationCapacity_i)^β × (Population_i)^γ, modelled on the World Bank's existing framework. Guillaumont, Guillaumont Jeanneney and Wagner (2017, *World Development*) argued for augmenting performance-based allocation with structural vulnerability and human capital indices, distinguishing exogenous needs from endogenous performance.

### Expert capture operates through accreditation barriers and donor influence

Climate adaptation finance exhibits expert capture at multiple levels. At the access level, the GCF accreditation process effectively excludes the most vulnerable. Of **62 developing-country institutions accredited for direct access, 42 had not received project funding** (WRI). Toko et al. (2022, *Climate Policy*) found the GCF lacks any unified framework for defining the "local level" — local actors are subjectively identified by accredited entities, most of which are large international organisations. Morgan and Petrou (2024) documented that SIDS have been proportionally less successful than other vulnerable groups in accessing GCF funding, because technical models embed assumptions about the benefits of "largeness" equated with value for money. Less than **17% of international public adaptation finance reaches local communities** (SEI/Canales & Savvidou, 2023).

At the governance level, Ciplet, Roberts and Khan (2013, *Global Environmental Politics*; 2015, MIT Press) identified three structural distortions: "The Gap" (insufficient funds), "The Wedge" (inequitable distribution), and "The Dodge" (governance by wealthy countries). Khan, Robinson, Weikmans and Roberts (2020, *Climatic Change*) showed that across 25 years of adaptation finance, wealthy countries have consistently promoted neoliberal justice frameworks that avoid measures evoking responsibility or liability. Roberts et al. (2021, *Nature Climate Change*) documented that the $100 billion pledge's deliberate indeterminacy about what counts as climate finance enabled donors to inflate reported figures — strategic misreporting by the more-trusted group.

### Decoupling evidence is overwhelming across all scales

The empirical evidence for decoupling in climate adaptation finance is the strongest across all three domains. **At the international level**, Weiler, Klöck and Dornan (2018, *World Development*) found that donors partly account for physical exposure but **countries with lower adaptive capacity do not receive more adaptation aid** — donor interests and governance quality override vulnerability. CPI (2024) found that international public adaptation finance to SIDS shows **no statistically significant correlation with climate vulnerability** (ND-GAIN scores), with 10 SIDS receiving 67% of tracked flows.

**At the subnational level**, Barrett's (2014, *World Development*) landmark study of adaptation finance in Malawi found areas most in need receive relatively little finance, with high socio-economic vulnerability showing a **negative relationship** with allocation. Barrett (2015, *Global Environmental Politics*) found similar patterns in Kenya, where political and economic preferences of decision-makers — not vulnerability levels — direct funding.

**At the systemic level**, the UNEP Adaptation Gap Report 2025 documents adaptation financing needs of **$310–365 billion per year by 2035** against current flows of $26 billion — a 12–14x gap. Only 66% of allocated adaptation funds are actually disbursed, compared to 98% for general development finance. The World Bank (2024) found countries affected by fragility and conflict receive significantly less adaptation finance despite urgent need, with subnational analysis showing finance does not flow to the most violent areas. A comprehensive multi-scalar review (2024, *Current Climate Change Reports*) concluded that climate vulnerability is **not a primary determinant of allocation at inter-state, subnational, or local scales** — institutional capacities, financial interests, and political considerations exert dominant influence.

### Strategic misreporting remains indirect but structurally predictable

Direct evidence of countries gaming vulnerability indices is scarce, but the conditions for strategic behaviour are well-documented. Lange, Löschel, Vogt and Ziegler (2010, *European Economic Review*) demonstrated that parties' support for equity rules in climate negotiations is correlated with their economic costs under each rule — equity arguments are deployed strategically. Different vulnerability indices produce different country rankings (Kim et al., 2019), and *Nature Climate Change* (2023) warned that adopting quantitative vulnerability measures for loss-and-damage allocation "is likely to bring division." Winsberg (2026, *Philosophy & Public Affairs*) identified perverse incentives in climate integration research: researchers include climate scenarios not because they find them reliable but because they are institutionally compelled, creating representational overextension that undermines the informational foundations of vulnerability assessment.

## 5. Cross-cutting synthesis: three structural patterns and five modelling gaps

### The same two failure modes recur with domain-specific mechanisms

Expert capture and decoupling are not independent pathologies but **systematically linked manifestations of the same structural vulnerability**: when the expert group both designs and operates the assessment technology that generates inputs to g(·), the lexicographic priority is undermined. Across domains, capture operates through five recurring mechanisms:

- **Structural conflicts of interest**: the entity assessing need is the entity that pays (insurance: *Glenn*; climate: accredited entities both propose and implement projects; welfare: caseworkers whose performance is measured by throughput)
- **Selection and compensation bias**: experts are chosen or rewarded by the more-trusted group's institutional adversaries (insurance: Unum's in-house physicians; welfare: performance-contingent provider contracts; climate: iTAP members from donor-aligned institutions)
- **Administrative complexity as a screening device**: Kleven and Kopczuk's formal result that optimal programmes feature high complexity and incomplete take-up is isomorphic to climate finance's accreditation barriers and welfare's administrative burden — in each case, **complexity filters out the most vulnerable**
- **Algorithmic amplification**: AI-driven utilisation review in health insurance (AMA data showing 16x denial rates), proxy means testing algorithms in welfare, and composite vulnerability index construction in climate finance all automate expert preferences into the assessment technology
- **Scale and capacity bias**: across all three domains, absorptive capacity and institutional quality are inversely correlated with need, and allocation mechanisms systematically favour capacity over vulnerability

Institutional decoupling theory provides the overarching framework. Meyer and Rowan (1977, *American Journal of Sociology*) showed organisations adopt formal structures ceremonially while maintaining "loose coupling" to actual operations. Bromley and Powell (2012, *Academy of Management Annals*) distinguished policy-practice decoupling (gap between adopted policy and implementation) from means-ends decoupling (gap between implemented practices and intended outcomes) — the latter being more prevalent and consequential. **Climate adaptation finance exhibits both**: policy-practice decoupling (vulnerability criteria adopted but not determinative of allocation) and means-ends decoupling (funded projects not reaching the most vulnerable).

Platteau (2004, *Development and Change*) and Platteau and Gaspart (2003, *World Development*) established that elite capture in community-driven development exploits information asymmetries and weak oversight, with rapid scaling being self-defeating. Bardhan and Mookherjee (2000, *American Economic Review*) formalised how local governance structures are vulnerable to capture by influential actors controlling public goods. These cross-domain findings converge with the domain-specific evidence: the two-group structure is inherently unstable because the expert group's information advantage allows systematic manipulation of the g(·) function.

### Five open formal modelling questions

The literature reveals substantial gaps that constitute a research frontier:

**First**, no integrated formal model captures the complete two-group structure with lexicographic priority. The claims-problem axiomatics (Thomson), bilevel optimisation (Dempe), and mechanism design with limited commitment (Doval-Skreta) each capture fragments, but no paper synthesises citizen-side welfare aggregation, expert-side constrained optimisation, and the conditions under which the lexicographic priority is maintained or violated.

**Second**, no game-theoretic model of strategic vulnerability reporting exists for climate adaptation finance. Despite extensive evidence that vulnerability definitions are politically contested and that equity rules are advocated self-interestedly (Lange et al., 2010), no formal model characterises the incentives for manipulation of vulnerability indicators or derives incentive-compatible allocation mechanisms.

**Third**, the absorptive capacity–vulnerability trade-off lacks formal optimisation theory. Empirically documented across climate finance (Barrett, 2014; Doshi & Garschagen, 2020; Weiler et al., 2018), the inverse correlation between vulnerability and implementation capacity creates a fundamental allocation dilemma for which no optimal rule has been characterised.

**Fourth**, no multi-level mechanism design framework addresses the "missing middle" — the documented gap between international climate funds and local implementation involves multiple principal-agent layers with endogenous information asymmetries at each level, but existing models treat at most two levels.

**Fifth**, the conditions under which ceremonial compliance and loose coupling emerge as equilibrium responses have not been formalised across domains. While institutional theory (Meyer & Rowan, Bromley & Powell) provides rich qualitative accounts, no optimisation model captures how decoupling arises endogenously from the interaction between formal needs-based allocation rules and the institutional incentives facing expert implementers.

## Conclusion

The literature reveals a profound structural irony: the theoretical tools to design needs-based allocation rules are well-developed (claims axiomatics, capabilities formalisation, optimal taxation theory), and the empirical evidence that these rules fail in practice is overwhelming (12–14x adaptation gap, zero vulnerability–allocation correlation for SIDS, 20–60% welfare non-take-up, 45% reversal of insurance denials). What is missing is the formal theory connecting these two bodies of work — a theory of **endogenous decoupling** that explains why expert groups systematically subvert the lexicographic priority despite formal institutional commitments to it. The mechanism design tradition's treatment of limited commitment (Doval & Skreta, 2022) and the bureaucratic agency literature (Prendergast, 2007; Kleven & Kopczuk, 2011) provide the most promising building blocks, but the synthesis remains an open problem. Climate adaptation finance, where the gap between formal vulnerability-based principles and actual allocation is most extensively documented and most consequential, offers the natural proving ground for such a theory.
