The Good Friday Agreement (1998) works across a [...] long [...] timescale. The gun — the Provisional IRA's military campaign and the loyalist counter-violence — had been loaded for nearly thirty years. What is structurally interesting is that the deescalation required not just the political agreement but a _series of explicit symbolic unloadings_: the decommissioning of weapons, witnessed by independent verifiers, culminating in General John de Chastelain's reports that the IRA had put its arms "beyond use." The language is precise and deliberate — not destroyed, but rendered permanently unfireable. The gun is not discarded dramatically but formally, ceremonially, explicitly disabled. This is perhaps the clearest real-world instance of your description of the object being destroyed to drive the probability of firing to zero. The ceremony of decommissioning _was_ the anti-Chekhov gesture made visible, because both communities needed to see the unloading.

The end of apartheid in South Africa (1990–94) presents a particularly instructive case because the gun had been loaded in _both_ directions: white minority anxiety about Black majority rule, and the ANC's armed wing Umkhonto we Sizwe, which had conducted a decades-long insurgency. The negotiated transition required both guns to be simultaneously and publicly unloaded — the unbanning of the ANC and release of Mandela were gestures of deescalation from one side; the ANC's suspension of armed struggle was the corresponding unloading from the other. What is remarkable is how explicitly this was managed as a symbolic choreography: each step was designed to reduce the probability of the other gun firing. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission is, in this reading, the literary epilogue — the account of all the guns that had already fired, told in a way designed to prevent re-loading.
