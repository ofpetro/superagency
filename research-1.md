# Failure modes of citizen-constrained expert optimization

**Two-stage institutions where citizens set a resource constraint and experts optimize within it are vulnerable to at least six distinct, formally characterized pathologies — and no known mechanism eliminates all of them simultaneously.** This structural form, exemplified by NICE's use of population-elicited QALY weights to set a cost-effectiveness threshold λ within which expert committees allocate, appears across health technology assessment, public budgeting, and delegated regulation. The theoretical literature in mechanism design, delegation theory, social choice, and health economics identifies precise conditions under which each pathology bites, and in several cases proposes partial remedies — but the impossibility results are binding enough that institutional designers face irreducible trade-offs. The most consequential practical finding is that NICE's operational threshold of £20,000–£30,000/QALY is roughly **twice the empirically estimated opportunity cost**, and the December 2025 UK-US trade deal raised it further, illustrating threshold manipulation in real time.

## Pathology 1: Strategic misreporting when reports determine a constraint

The foundational concern is whether citizens truthfully report preferences when they know reports feed a downstream constraint. The answer depends sharply on the dimensionality and domain of the aggregation problem.

**The Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem** (Gibbard 1973; Satterthwaite 1975) establishes that any surjective, non-dictatorial social choice function with three or more outcomes in its range is manipulable — some voter can benefit from misreporting. However, this impossibility applies to ordinal SCFs over discrete outcome sets. When the mechanism's output is a single scalar (a budget level or threshold), and citizen preferences are single-peaked over that scalar, the impossibility dissolves. **Moulin (1980)** proved that generalized median voter rules are the unique class of anonymous, Pareto-efficient, strategy-proof SCFs on single-peaked domains. Under the pure median rule, no citizen can benefit by misreporting their preferred threshold: any deviation either has no effect or moves the outcome away from their ideal point. This is the strongest positive result for Stage 1 design.

The escape fails in three important cases. First, when preferences are **not single-peaked** — citizens who would opt out of a public system at intermediate funding levels, or who have non-monotone preferences over the threshold, reintroduce manipulability. Second, when the budget decision is **multi-dimensional** (allocating across programs), Border and Jordan (1983) showed that only dictatorial rules are strategy-proof for non-separable preferences. Third, and most subtly, even when Stage 1 alone is strategy-proof, **the composed mechanism** (report → constraint → expert allocation → final outcome) may not be. If citizens understand how the threshold maps through expert optimization to their personal outcomes, they face a reduced-form game whose incentive properties differ from Stage 1 in isolation.

In quasilinear environments, **VCG mechanisms** (Clarke 1971; Groves 1973) provide dominant-strategy truthful elicitation, but at the cost of **budget imbalance** — the Green-Laffont (1977) impossibility shows that no mechanism simultaneously achieves efficiency, strategy-proofness, and balanced budgets. Wagner and Meir (2023) recently constructed a VCG-like mechanism for participatory budgeting where citizens determine both total expenditure and allocation; critically, the required side-payments vanish as population size grows, making the mechanism practical for large-scale settings like health systems. For well-being reports specifically, proper scoring rules (Brier, logarithmic) incentivize truthful probabilistic forecasts but require a verifiable ground truth — which subjective well-being lacks. Surrogate scoring rules using peer prediction (Shnayder et al. 2022) partially address this but remain untested in HTA contexts.

**Empirical evidence:** No published study has directly tested whether citizens strategically misreport QALY valuations when aware of scarcity implications. However, the Moretti et al. (2018) finding that pharmaceutical companies submit significantly higher ICERs to NICE (which has an explicit threshold) than to PBAC (which does not) demonstrates that **the existence of a known threshold induces strategic behavior** by at least one party in the system.

**Known remedies:** Median aggregation for scalar thresholds; VCG with vanishing payments for large populations; domain restriction to single-peaked preferences through deliberative processes; iterative expert-citizen feedback to align reported preferences with informed judgments.

## Pathology 2: Aggregation distortion from preference heterogeneity

Even absent strategic behavior, aggregating heterogeneous well-being reports into a single resource level R necessarily distorts. The distortion's character depends entirely on the aggregation rule chosen.

**Arrow's Impossibility Theorem** (1951) establishes that no ordinal aggregation rule with unrestricted domain simultaneously satisfies Weak Pareto, Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives, and Non-Dictatorship across three or more alternatives. For single-dimensional budget decisions, this impossibility is escaped via domain restriction (Black 1948; Moulin 1980), but a different problem emerges: **the median rule ignores preference intensity**. A citizen whose life depends on R = 100 counts equally with one who mildly prefers R = 100. The gap between R_median and the utilitarian optimum R_util grows with the skewness of the preference distribution and with heterogeneity in marginal utilities.

**Harsanyi's Aggregation Theorem** (1955) shows that if individual and social preferences satisfy VNM expected utility axioms and a Pareto condition, the social welfare function must be an affine combination of individual utilities — formally utilitarian. But Sen's critique (1970, 1976) identifies the fatal flaw: VNM utilities measure **attitudes toward risk**, not welfare. Summing them across individuals conflates risk-preference strength with welfare importance. Roberts (1980) proved that **cardinal unit comparability** (CUC) — utilities comparable in differences across persons — is necessary and sufficient to pin down weighted utilitarianism under Arrovian axioms. Without CUC, cardinal reports do not escape impossibility. This is directly relevant to QALY aggregation: QALYs represent a utilitarian sum of cardinal health utilities, but the interpersonal comparability assumption is empirically unsupported.

**Romer and Rosenthal (1978, 1979)** demonstrated that even with single-peaked preferences, institutional structure matters: an agenda setter exploiting a low reversion point can extract budgets above the median voter's ideal. In their "setter model," a budget-maximizing agency proposes R such that the median voter is just indifferent between R and the status quo — systematically inflating R whenever the reversion level is low. This is directly analogous to HTA agencies that frame the alternative to a higher threshold as "no access to new treatments."

**Interaction with downstream optimization:** When the expert receives a distorted R̂ instead of the true optimum R*, welfare loss depends on curvature. Under standard concavity (diminishing returns), the loss is second-order: L ≈ ½|W″(R*)| × (R̂ − R*)², meaning expert optimization partially compensates. But when the allocation problem has **threshold effects or complementarities** — a minimum viable budget below which a programme cannot function, or O-ring-style quality interactions — aggregation error is amplified catastrophically through Stage 2.

## Pathology 3: Delegation bias and compound information loss

The optimal delegation literature, initiated by **Holmström (1977/1984)** and formalized by **Alonso and Matouschek (2008)**, characterizes what happens when a principal restricts a biased agent's action set. In the canonical model with quadratic loss and uniform states, the optimal delegation set is a **threshold/cap**: D* = [0, 1−2b], where b is the agent's bias. The agent gets full flexibility below the cap but cannot implement extreme actions. Greater bias → tighter cap; greater state uncertainty → wider delegation. The core trade-off is **information loss** (tight constraints prevent the agent from using private information) versus **bias control** (loose constraints let the agent exploit discretion).

In the three-party structure (citizens → constraint → expert), **Liang (2013)** extends this to hierarchies with a strategic intermediary. When the constraint-setter's preferences differ from both the principal's and the agent's, two new pathologies emerge. First, **compounding bias**: each layer introduces its own preference distortion, and the final action may deviate from the principal's ideal more than in a two-party problem. Second, **multi-interval delegation sets**: the optimal constraint may have "holes," creating policy discontinuities where small changes in the underlying state cause jumps in the expert's action. This is structurally analogous to threshold effects in HTA where marginal changes in an ICER around λ cause binary accept/reject outcomes.

**Dessein (2002)** showed that in the binary choice between centralized communication (cheap talk) and full delegation, **delegation dominates whenever informative communication is feasible** — the informational loss from cheap talk grows faster than the control loss from delegation. The quantitative result is striking: as preference alignment increases, the principal's expected loss under communication is infinitely larger than under delegation. This provides a formal justification for the two-stage structure: letting experts decide within a citizen-determined constraint is generically better than having citizens advise experts who then decide freely.

**Amador and Bagwell (2013)** generalized the delegation framework in *Econometrica*, providing sufficient conditions for interval delegation (cap rules) to be optimal even when money burning (procedural costs) is feasible. Their main result: under appropriate concavity conditions on the principal's welfare function and non-decreasing density of states, **simple caps without money burning achieve the optimum**. This supports the design principle behind NICE-style thresholds — a ceiling on acceptable ICER — as a theoretically optimal form of delegation, provided the ceiling is set correctly.

**The critical vulnerability** is that in the motivating application, the ceiling is not set by the principal (society) through an optimal mechanism but is determined by a separate aggregation process subject to all the pathologies in Sections 1 and 2. The delegation literature assumes the principal chooses the optimal constraint; here, the constraint emerges from a separate, potentially distorted process.

## Pathology 4: Threshold capture, lobbying, and endogenous manipulation

The cost-effectiveness threshold λ is itself a choice variable subject to political pressure. The Laffont-Tirole (1991) model of regulatory capture provides the formal framework: in a three-tier hierarchy (Congress → Regulator → Firm), the regulated firm can bribe or influence the regulator to conceal unfavorable information, and the optimal response requires **reducing the power of incentive contracts** to lower the stakes of collusion. Translated to HTA: pharmaceutical firms lobby to raise λ, which increases the set of approvable drugs and thus industry revenue.

**Claxton et al. (2015)** estimated the NHS marginal cost per QALY — the empirical opportunity cost threshold — at **£12,936**, with P(threshold < £20,000) = 0.89. NICE's operational range of £20,000–£30,000 is thus roughly 1.5–2.3× the evidence-based level. At the £30,000 threshold, a drug costing £10M/year generates 333 QALYs but displaces an estimated 773 QALYs elsewhere in cancer, circulatory, and respiratory care. The threshold was never empirically derived; it was set administratively at NICE's 1999 inception and remained unchanged for over two decades. McCabe, Claxton, and Culyer (2008) concluded bluntly: "the uncomfortable truth is that NICE's threshold has no basis in either theory or evidence."

The December 2025 UK-US pharmaceutical trade deal provides a **textbook case of threshold endogeneity**: the UK government raised NICE's threshold from £20,000–£30,000 to £25,000–£35,000/QALY as part of securing zero tariffs on UK pharmaceutical exports. This moved the threshold in exactly the opposite direction from Claxton's evidence-based recommendation. The ABPI, funded by pharmaceutical companies, had lobbied for a threshold increase. NICE projected 3–5 additional annual drug approvals and £1.5 billion in additional NHS spending by 2028 — costs borne as displaced health outcomes for existing patients.

Cross-country evidence reinforces the pattern. **WHO-CHOICE** recommended thresholds of 1–3× GDP per capita, which Woods, Revill, Sculpher, and Claxton (2016) showed to be arbitrary and massively too high for low- and middle-income countries. For Malawi, the opportunity-cost threshold is $3–$116 versus WHO's suggestion of $226–$678. WHO subsequently withdrew the recommendation. Germany's IQWiG uses indication-specific efficiency frontiers rather than a generic threshold, avoiding cross-indication rationing but creating complex uncertainties about where the frontier falls — and the method requires at least two existing comparators, making it inapplicable for first-in-class drugs.

**Endogenous threshold effects on market behavior** have been formally modeled: a game-theoretic analysis (ScienceDirect, 2023) shows that a stricter threshold can induce incumbent drug producers to switch from entry accommodation to entry deterrence, and is "never pro-competitive" — it may facilitate collusive outcomes with higher prices for both drugs. This means the threshold is not merely a passive constraint but **actively shapes the strategic landscape** it is supposed to evaluate.

**Known remedies:** Claxton's opportunity-cost estimation approach with regular updating; McCabe et al.'s proposal for an independent threshold committee analogous to the Monetary Policy Committee; separation of scientific assessment from pricing authority (Towse 2010); transparency requirements on pharmaceutical industry engagement with threshold-setting processes.

## Pathology 5: Collusion across the hierarchy

**Tirole (1986)** established that in three-layer hierarchies (principal → supervisor → agent), the supervisor and agent may collude — the supervisor conceals information about the agent's type in exchange for side-payments. **Laffont and Martimort (1997)** showed that preventing collusion under asymmetric information with correlated types requires strict efficiency losses: the principal must distort allocations to eliminate the gains from side-contracting. With uncorrelated types, collusion prevention is costless; with correlated types, it is not.

In the citizen-constraint-expert structure, three collusion channels exist. First, **expert-industry collusion**: the expert committee may collude with technology suppliers (pharmaceutical firms) to approve marginal technologies, sharing the rents through revolving-door employment, research funding, or cognitive capture (Dal Bó 2006 catalogues incentive-based, informational, cognitive, and coercive capture mechanisms). Second, **citizen-expert collusion**: patient advocacy groups (partially industry-funded) may coordinate with sympathetic committee members to inflate valuations of specific health states, shifting the threshold or the within-constraint allocation. Third, **citizen-industry collusion**: industry may fund patient advocacy organizations that coach patients to report higher QALY impacts, thereby inflating the aggregate constraint.

**Laffont and Martimort (1998)** proved a counterintuitive result: **delegation may be preferred precisely because it is more robust to collusion** than centralization. Under incomplete contracting (communication limits), centralized mechanisms are vulnerable to collusion because anonymity conditions create conflicts between participation and coalition incentive constraints. Delegation, being inherently non-anonymous and asymmetric, can avoid this conflict. This provides a second formal justification for the two-stage structure — not just as optimal delegation but as a collusion-resistant institutional form.

**Empirical evidence:** US pharmaceutical industry spending on federal lobbying totaled $4.7 billion from 1999–2018 (Wouters 2020, JAMA Internal Medicine), exceeding all other industries. In the UK, the complex web of interests connecting NICE committee members, the OHE (funded by the ABPI), and academic health economists illustrates the capture channels. Devlin and Parkin (2004) found NICE's revealed threshold was approximately **£45,000/QALY** — far above the stated £20,000–£30,000 — suggesting the committee systematically exercised discretion to approve technologies that breached the nominal constraint.

## Pathology 6: Measurement bias in the citizen-reporting instrument

Even if the aggregation mechanism and institutional structure were perfect, the **input data** — QALY valuations elicited from the general population — are systematically biased in at least four documented ways.

**Decision utility versus experienced utility.** Dolan and Kahneman (2008) demonstrated that general public respondents imagining health states suffer from **focusing illusions**: they overweight the disability and ignore unaffected life dimensions. Patients actually experiencing chronic conditions consistently rate their quality of life higher than healthy respondents imagining those conditions — the "disability paradox" (Menzel, Dolan, Richardson, and Olsen 2002). Sackett and Torrance (1978) found home dialysis patients rated their QoL at 0.56 versus 0.39 from public representatives, yielding QALY gains of 0.44 versus 0.61 per year. This systematic bias means population-elicited QALY weights **overestimate the disutility of chronic conditions**, inflating the apparent benefit of treatments and biasing the threshold upward.

**Scope insensitivity and embedding effects.** QALY valuations exhibit the standard Kahneman scope insensitivity: exponential increases in health risk produce only linear increases in willingness to pay. Dolan, Shaw, Tsuchiya, and Williams (2005) found that social values diminish at the margin for both quality and length of life, violating the QALY maximization assumption of linearity. Furthermore, valuations depend on characteristics beyond QALY-generating capacity — worse lifetime health prospects and having dependents both increase social value, in ways QALYs cannot capture.

**Elicitation method artifacts.** Time trade-off (TTO) and standard gamble (SG) yield systematically different utilities due to probability weighting, loss aversion, and scale compatibility (Bleichrodt 2002). SG produces upward-biased values from probability weighting; TTO has partially offsetting biases. These are not random errors but systematic distortions that propagate through threshold-setting. Country-specific EQ-5D value sets differ substantially due to different valuation protocols, meaning an identical intervention may cross the threshold in one country but not another based purely on the measurement instrument.

**Implications for the two-stage structure:** These biases mean the citizen-reported inputs to Stage 1 are corrupted before aggregation even begins. The threshold λ derived from biased QALY weights is itself biased — typically upward, given the focusing illusion. When this inflated λ is passed to Stage 2, the expert committee operates within a constraint that is systematically too generous, approving technologies that displace more health than they create.

**Known remedies:** Supplementing decision utility with experienced utility measures (day reconstruction method, experience sampling); bias correction for probability weighting and loss aversion in TTO/SG (Lipman et al. 2020); validation modules allowing respondents to confirm or adjust initial valuations; using patient-reported values alongside general population values; abandoning "states worse than dead" (negative QALY values) on ethical grounds.

## The eight essential papers

A researcher entering this area should read the following first, in approximate priority order:

- **Moulin (1980)**, "On strategy-proofness and single-peakedness," *Public Choice* — proves median rules are the unique strategy-proof, anonymous, efficient mechanisms for single-dimensional aggregation, establishing the best-case scenario for Stage 1
- **Alonso and Matouschek (2008)**, "Optimal Delegation," *Review of Economic Studies* — characterizes optimal constraint design for biased agents, providing the formal framework for Stage 2
- **Claxton et al. (2015)**, "Methods for the estimation of the NICE cost-effectiveness threshold," *Health Technology Assessment* — the definitive empirical estimate of the opportunity-cost threshold, demonstrating the pathology of a politically set λ
- **Laffont and Tirole (1991)**, "The Politics of Government Decision-Making: A Theory of Regulatory Capture," *QJE* — the canonical three-tier model of capture in hierarchical regulation, directly applicable to expert committee capture
- **Dolan and Kahneman (2008)**, "Interpretations of utility and their implications for the valuation of health," *Economic Journal* — establishes that decision-utility-based QALY elicitation systematically misrepresents well-being
- **Harsanyi (1955)**, "Cardinal welfare, individualistic ethics, and interpersonal comparisons of utility," *Journal of Political Economy* — the foundational theorem connecting expected utility, Pareto, and utilitarian aggregation, with implications for QALY-based welfare
- **Mookherjee (2006)**, "Decentralization, Hierarchies, and Incentives: A Mechanism Design Perspective," *Journal of Economic Literature* — the definitive survey on when delegation replicates or falls short of centralization
- **Amador and Bagwell (2013)**, "The Theory of Optimal Delegation with an Application to Tariff Caps," *Econometrica* — generalizes delegation theory with sufficient conditions for interval delegation, providing formal support for cap-style thresholds

## Conclusion: Irreducible trade-offs in two-stage institutional design

The six pathologies interact in ways that make comprehensive remediation impossible. Moulin's theorem tells us that scalar threshold aggregation can be strategy-proof under single-peaked preferences — but the QALY measurement biases documented by Dolan and Kahneman mean the reported preferences fed into that mechanism are corrupted inputs. Alonso-Matouschek and Amador-Bagwell tell us that cap-style delegation is optimal when the cap is correctly set — but Claxton's evidence shows the cap is set at roughly twice the opportunity cost, and the 2025 trade deal demonstrates that the cap is endogenous to political bargaining rather than welfare-maximizing design.

The deepest tension is between **democratic legitimacy** (citizens determine the constraint) and **epistemic quality** (the constraint reflects actual welfare). The delegation literature shows that this tension is not merely practical but structural: the Revelation Principle (Myerson 1981) guarantees that any two-stage outcome is replicable by a centralized mechanism, but Mookherjee (2006) identifies three conditions — communication costs, contract complexity limits, and collusion — under which the two-stage structure is genuinely superior. The normative priority that the resource constraint be citizen-determined is thus not merely a democratic preference but can be justified on mechanism-design grounds when these conditions hold. The challenge is ensuring that what citizens determine reflects their actual welfare rather than the product of focusing illusions, industry lobbying, and political convenience.
