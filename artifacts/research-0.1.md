# Structurally Separated Expert Allocation Bodies with Self-Interest: A Stringent Case Survey

## TL;DR
- **Three cases satisfy the full structural pattern with strong empirical and theoretical support**: (1) U.S. Regional Fishery Management Council Scientific and Statistical Committees (SSCs) setting Acceptable Biological Catch limits, (2) final-offer "baseball" arbitration (the Ashenfelter–Bloom "statistical exchangeability" model), and (3) the Appointed Actuary / peer-review regime for insurance reserving (e.g., Canada's OSFI Guideline E-15, U.S. ASOPs).
- **Two further cases qualify with caveats**: pension/endowment trustee fiduciary regimes (strong on separation but the welfare-envelope link is weaker), and crisis-standards-of-care triage committees (strong on separation and stabilisation but the envelope is fixed, not driven by reported welfare).
- **Three frequently-cited candidates fail or are genuinely ambiguous**: hospital P&T (formulary) committees (literature documents that COIs *do* shift decisions, so stabilisation is contested), internal corporate capital allocation (winner-picking is theorised by Stein 1997 but empirics show pervasive influence-cost gaming), and UK REF panels (separation is partial because panellists come from assessed institutions).

---

## Key Findings

The cleanest match for the requested structural pattern occurs where (a) a *technical certification* of a population's welfare drives a budget envelope, (b) the certifier is professionally rather than locally embedded, and (c) a *repeated-game* selection mechanism penalises systematic bias. Final-offer arbitration is the analytically purest case — there is a formal theorem behind why honesty is stabilised. Fishery SSCs are the most institutionally rich case, with the welfare envelope (Total Allowable Catch) directly tied to the experts' scientific output. The actuarial regime is the most professionally codified.

The weaker fits all stumble on the same point: either the experts have *traceable* career stakes in particular subjects (REF panellists, divisional managers), or the welfare measurement drives only *internal distribution* and not the size of the envelope (triage, endowment spending), or empirical evidence simply does not support the "stabilisation" claim (P&T committees with pharma ties, Scharfstein–Stein "socialism" in conglomerates).

## Details

### Case 1 — U.S. Regional Fishery Management Council Scientific and Statistical Committees (SSCs)

**Structure.** The reporting "subject" is each managed fish stock, whose welfare is measured by stock assessments (biomass, fishing mortality, recruitment). Under the 2007 reauthorisation of the Magnuson–Stevens Act, an SSC — a body of academic, state, and federal scientists appointed by each of the eight regional councils — recommends an Overfishing Limit (OFL) and an Acceptable Biological Catch (ABC). By statute, **the Council's Annual Catch Limit (ACL) cannot exceed the SSC's ABC.** The assessment of stock welfare therefore *directly determines the size of the resource envelope* available to fishers, satisfying the strongest version of requirement (4).

**Separation.** SSC members are formally distinct from the harvesting/processing fishers who are the ultimate downstream beneficiaries (or losers). Magnuson–Stevens §302(g) requires every SSC member to file NOAA Form 88-195 disclosing any financial interest in harvesting, processing, lobbying, advocacy or marketing in any council-managed fishery. SSC members may serve on Council committees but may not be employed by an interest or advocacy group. North Pacific Council Standard Operating Procedures explicitly distinguish the SSC's role from industry advisory panels.

**Expert self-interest.** This is real but diffuse. SSC members are mostly stock-assessment scientists whose research funding (often from NOAA Fisheries Science Centers, Sea Grant, or industry-adjacent foundations like the Bering Sea Fisheries Research Foundation) and professional reputations are bound up in fisheries science. They have *domain-wide* career stakes in the credibility of stock assessments, but not traceable stakes in specific stock-by-stock outcomes for specific fishers. Direction of potential gaming is bidirectional: members could under- or over-state stock health.

**Evidence of stabilisation.** Empirically, NOAA Fisheries reports — over 47 stocks declared rebuilt and overfished-stock numbers near all-time lows in the 2017 and subsequent Status of Stocks reports — indicate the system constrains overfishing despite political pressure on the Council level. The 2014 ICES Journal of Marine Science paper (Methot et al.) documents that since the SSC was empowered to set the binding ABC ceiling, the gap between scientific advice and political catch limits has effectively been closed in U.S. federal waters — a striking contrast to the EU Council of Ministers, which between 2010 and 2017 exceeded ICES advice on roughly 60% of TACs analysed. The contrast itself is suggestive: where the expert body has *binding* authority and is structurally separated from harvesters, stabilisation is observed; where it is merely advisory, it is not.

**Mechanism.** Three reinforcing structural features: (i) statutory binding authority of the SSC's ABC; (ii) financial disclosure with annual refile and recusal; (iii) layered peer review of stock assessments through Stock Assessment and Fishery Evaluation reports and SEDAR-style panels. Theoretical treatment is partial — treated as good administrative-law design rather than formally modelled.

**Verdict: STRONG FIT.** All four structural requirements satisfied, with the strongest version of (4).

### Case 2 — Final-Offer Arbitration (MLB Salary, New Jersey Police, Chilean Special Collective Bargaining)

**Structure.** Reporting subjects are players/employees (welfare = compensation); experts are arbitrators chosen from a register. In MLB salary arbitration, three-arbitrator panels select either the player's or the team's submitted salary figure — no compromise allowed. The arbitrator's choice determines that player's salary for the upcoming season.

**Separation.** Arbitrators are private professionals (lawyers, economists) with no current membership in either the player or club community. They are paid per case and have no traceable post-term stake in any individual player or team.

**Expert self-interest.** Direct and material — arbitrators are paid per appointment, and selection in future cases depends on their prior record. This is precisely the "career stakes" the question targets.

**Evidence of stabilisation.** This is the rare case with a *formal theoretical model* of why honesty is stabilised. The Ashenfelter–Bloom "statistical exchangeability" hypothesis (Ashenfelter & Bloom 1984; Ashenfelter 1987; Ashenfelter, Currie, Farber & Spiegel 1992 in *Econometrica*) holds: because both parties take turns striking arbitrators from a list, any arbitrator perceptibly biased toward one side will be vetoed by the other and lose future appointments. The competitive selection process drives arbitrators to converge on the "impartial" award. Empirically, Ashenfelter–Dahl (2005) and subsequent New Jersey police-arbitration tests fail to reject statistical exchangeability — arbitrator fixed effects are jointly insignificant once case characteristics are controlled. MLB arbitration data show clubs winning ~57% of hearings, but Dartmouth thesis evidence and Faurot–McAllister (1992, *ILR Review*) show this reflects asymmetric risk preferences and CBA-listed criteria, not arbitrator bias.

**Mechanism.** Repeated-game competitive selection, with both parties holding equal information and equal striking power. The mechanism breaks down precisely when one party becomes informationally advantaged — Egan, Matvos & Seru (2018, NBER WP 25150) prove that in *consumer* arbitration the same exchangeability logic produces pro-firm bias because consumers cannot strike intelligently. This negative result actually reinforces the positive case: stabilisation is a property of the bilateral selection structure, not of arbitrator virtue.

**Welfare-drives-envelope?** Partial. The arbitrator chooses among offers within a bounded budget; the *level* of compensation does not strictly drive a separate budget envelope. This is the weakest aspect of the fit. However, in aggregate, arbitration outcomes set comparable-salary baselines that anchor the entire arbitration-eligible class, which Brown & Jepsen (2009) and others show feeds into team payroll envelopes.

**Verdict: STRONG FIT** on stabilisation theory and separation; **partial fit** on requirement (4). The case is analytically distinctive because it has a real theorem behind it.

### Case 3 — Appointed Actuary Regime and Actuarial Peer Review (Insurance Reserving and Pricing)

**Structure.** Reporting subjects are policyholders (and indirectly shareholders/regulators), whose collective welfare is captured in claim-cost development, mortality experience, and reserve adequacy. The Appointed Actuary (AA) — typically Fellow of the Casualty Actuarial Society or Society of Actuaries — values policy liabilities, signs the statement of actuarial opinion, and feeds into regulatory capital tests (LICAT/LIMAT/MCT in Canada; RBC in the U.S.). These valuations directly determine how much capital must be held against future claims, which in turn drives premium-setting and underwriting capacity — i.e., the *resource envelope* available for new business.

**Separation.** The AA is structurally separated from claims adjusters, underwriters, and in particular from the salesforce. Under Canada's OSFI Guideline E-15 (revised 2023), peer reviewers must be independent from the AA, and OSFI expects the peer reviewer not to be from the same external audit firm. Peer reviews continue under the 2023 guideline through 2026; OSFI's 2027 elimination is on the basis that IFRS 17 and standard-setting now provide adequate substitutes.

**Expert self-interest.** Substantial and career-traceable but *not* policyholder-specific: actuaries' compensation, advancement, and continued Fellowship qualification depend on producing reserves that satisfy management (which prefers lower reserves to free capital) *and* regulators (who prefer adequate reserves) *and* auditors. CAS Actuarial Review (Gross, "Fighting Confirmation Bias in Loss Reserving") explicitly documents the directional pressure toward management-preferred numbers. The Stein paper "Sources of Bias and Inaccuracy in the Development of a Best Estimate" (CAS Forum) is an in-house literature on this.

**Evidence of stabilisation.** Multiple reinforcing mechanisms: (i) Actuarial Standards of Practice (ASOP 36, ASOP 43) compelled by ASB and binding through Code of Professional Conduct; (ii) U.S. Qualification Standards mandating bias-topic CE since 2022; (iii) mandatory external peer review under OSFI E-15 (2003–2026); (iv) Disciplinary tribunals at CIA, AAA, and other bodies; (v) the "appointed actuary" itself is a regulatorily designated role with statutory duties. Empirical evidence on bias is mixed but reserving studies (Casualty Actuarial Society) generally find that systematic, profession-wide bias is small relative to the magnitude of the discretionary range.

**Mechanism.** Professional certification with credible disciplinary threat + mandatory documented peer review by an independent qualified actuary + regulatory review of opinions. This is a textbook "professional norms + repeated-game reputation" stabilisation, formalised in actuarial literature.

**Verdict: STRONG FIT.** All four requirements satisfied, including the welfare-drives-envelope condition through the link between reserves and underwriting capacity.

### Case 4 — Pension and Endowment Investment Committees (Trust-Law Fiduciary Regime)

**Structure.** Reporting subjects are plan beneficiaries (pension) or charitable purposes (endowment). Investment committees of trustees set asset allocation, spending rates, and manager selection. For ERISA pensions, the "sole interest rule" (29 U.S.C. §1104(a)(1)) mandates fiduciaries discharge duties "solely in the interest of the participants and beneficiaries." Public pension trustees operate under analogous state law. University endowment investment committees (e.g., Brown's, governed under UPMIFA) operate under prudent-investor and impartiality duties.

**Separation.** Trustees are typically not current beneficiaries (or, for university endowments, not current operating-budget recipients). Cambridge Associates' governance papers stress that the investment committee should be structurally separate from the finance committee that consumes the spending distribution.

**Expert self-interest.** This is the weakest aspect of the case. Trustees of major endowments are unpaid; their stake is reputational/social-status. Pension trustees are sometimes compensated and may have political-career stakes (especially elected public-pension trustees). The Harvard Law CGFR analyses and Langbein (Yale Law) discuss this in depth.

**Evidence of stabilisation.** The trust-law duty of loyalty is *prophylactically* enforced — courts find breach for self-dealing transactions even absent demonstrated harm. ERISA fiduciary disclosures, periodic actuarial valuation, and external auditor review all reinforce the regime. However, GAO (GAO-07-703) and SEC studies have flagged real conflicts among pension consultants, and Indiana AG Opinion 2022-3 illustrates ongoing legal contestation. Stabilisation is institutionally claimed but not uncontested in empirical literature.

**Welfare-drives-envelope?** Indirect. The endowment's value drives the spending envelope mechanically through the spending rule, but the committee's job is allocation *within* that envelope. The link between committee discretion and the size of the budget for beneficiaries is real but mediated.

**Verdict: GOOD FIT WITH CAVEATS.** Separation and the formal mechanism are clean; welfare-envelope link is mechanical rather than discretionary; empirical stabilisation evidence is mixed. The Texas Law Review piece by Hirst & Robertson and Harvard CGFR posts are useful theoretical treatments.

### Case 5 — Crisis Standards of Care (CSC) Triage Committees

**Structure.** During pandemic surge or disaster, hospital triage committees (typically three-clinician panels distinct from the patient's treating team — e.g., URMC's Coronavirus Ethics Response Group; UC Davis's Triage Officer/Team model; New York State's 2015 Ventilator Allocation Guidelines) score patients via SOFA + comorbidity-based protocols and assign priority for ventilators, ICU beds, ECMO.

**Separation.** This is the strongest aspect: virtually every published CSC framework explicitly mandates that the triage officer cannot be the patient's clinician, and most stipulate that appeals go to a separate Hospital Ethics Committee.

**Expert self-interest.** Career-status stakes in being seen as competent triage clinicians, plus profession-wide reputation. *Not* tied to specific patient outcomes (in fact the structural separation is designed to rule that out).

**Evidence of stabilisation.** Bhavani et al., Wunsch et al., and the Rochester simulation work document that protocols, when followed, produce defensible distributions across racial and clinical groups — though questions remain about SOFA's predictive power for COVID-19. Truog (Hastings Cent Rep 2021) is more sceptical that any of these protocols actually outperform clinical judgment, but the *honesty* of the panel — i.e., absence of self-dealing — is largely uncontested.

**Welfare-drives-envelope?** No. The number of ventilators is fixed during a crisis; the committee allocates within. This fails the strong version of requirement (4).

**Verdict: PARTIAL FIT.** Strong on separation and self-interest stabilisation; fails on envelope-driving.

## Cases Flagged as Ambiguous (and why)

### Hospital Pharmacy & Therapeutics (P&T) Committees
The Friesen, Caplan & Miller (2020) *J Clinical Pharmacy & Therapeutics* review of P&T COIs concludes that pharmaceutical-industry ties measurably influence formulary decisions — physicians who interact with pharma reps are more likely to request additions of those firms' products. The literature is essentially a documentation of *failure* of stabilisation. Including this case would misrepresent the evidence; better to flag explicitly that **structural separation is not honoured** when committee members have ongoing financial relationships with manufacturers, and the empirical record runs against the stabilisation claim.

### Internal Capital Allocation in Multidivisional Firms
Stein's (1997 *Journal of Finance*) winner-picking model is a *theoretical* case for headquarters value-creation through reallocation. But the empirical literature (Scharfstein & Stein 2000 "The Dark Side of Internal Capital Markets"; Rajan, Servaes & Zingales 2000; Meyer, Milgrom & Roberts 1992 on influence costs) consistently finds *socialism* — funds flow toward weaker divisions, connected managers extract investment unrelated to opportunities, and influence activities are pervasive. Jandik et al. (2025, *JBFA*) find that *connectedness* among divisional managers actually mitigates misallocation, which speaks to a different mechanism. The case violates the question's evidentiary bar: stabilisation is theorised but not robustly empirically observed at the level of the typical conglomerate.

### NIH Study Sections / Academic Grant Peer Review
The structural pattern is approximately right — applicants are universities/PIs whose collective scientific productivity arguably underwrites NIH appropriations, and reviewers are senior PIs from a different institution — but *separation* is genuinely partial. Reviewers are themselves grant applicants in adjacent cycles, and the same individuals will be applicants and reviewers across overlapping windows. Recusal handles the application-specific COI but not the diffuse "I am a member of the funded community" stake. CIHR's International Peer Review Expert Panel (Gallo et al. 2016) found roughly one-third of reviewers had at least one conflict, with only 35% self-reported. PNAS evidence (Pier et al. 2018) of low inter-rater agreement does not by itself imply gaming, but it complicates any claim of stabilised honesty. **Conclusion: the *applicants-are-also-reviewers* feature means this is a "distributed peer review" pattern more than a strict separation case.**

### REF (UK Research Excellence Framework) Panels
Drives a £2 billion QR funding envelope, satisfies (4) in the strongest form. But REF panellists are senior academics from the very same UK institutions being assessed; recusal is by submission, not blanket. Separation is institutionally administered rather than structural. The Stern review and Aboubichr & Conway (2023) on PM gaming in British universities document significant strategic responses. **Better classified as a *managed-COI* regime than a true separation case.**

### Mondragon Governing Council
Explicitly excluded by the question, but worth noting that the Governing Council members *are themselves worker-members during their term* — separation fails the question's stringent requirement.

### Public Utility Rate-Setting Commissions
Commissioners are politically appointed and conduct quasi-judicial proceedings to set rates. Reporting subjects are utilities (and ratepayers); the welfare measurement (allowed return, cost of service) drives the rate envelope. *However*, commissioners are typically heavily statutorily constrained, with formal cost-of-service formulas — this risks failing requirement (2) (meaningful expert discretion). Also, commissioner career paths are often traceable to specific industries (revolving door), which compromises (1). I have not included it in the strong cases but it is worth a closer look in any deeper inquiry.

### EU Common Fisheries Policy / ICES
Excluded as transnational per the question's scale rule, but is an instructive *negative* control: ICES advice is not binding on the Council of Ministers, who exceeded scientific advice on ~60% of TACs 2010–2017. This contrast with U.S. SSCs is the cleanest natural experiment in the survey on the importance of *binding* expert authority.

## Recommendations

If the goal is to identify the cleanest, most analytically tractable instances of the structural pattern for further theoretical or empirical work, prioritise as follows:

1. **Start with final-offer arbitration.** It has the only formal theorem in the set (Ashenfelter–Bloom statistical exchangeability), it has been empirically tested across MLB, New Jersey police, and Canadian/Chilean labour contexts, and the Egan–Matvos–Seru (2018) consumer-arbitration negative result usefully sharpens the conditions under which the mechanism works (bilateral information and bilateral striking power). If the underlying theoretical question is *what stabilises honest expert allocation under self-interest*, this is the case where a generative answer already exists.

2. **Use U.S. Regional Fishery Council SSCs as the institutional benchmark.** It uniquely satisfies the strong-form welfare-drives-envelope requirement, has a clean institutional cousin (EU/ICES) for natural-experiment comparisons, and has a publicly observable success record (rebuilt stocks, overfished count near historic lows). The 2007 Magnuson–Stevens reauthorisation that made SSC ABC binding is itself a quasi-experimental shock worth exploiting.

3. **Treat the actuarial regime as the canonical professionalised case.** It is the example where stabilisation is achieved primarily through *certification + standards + mandatory peer review*, with relatively little structural-separation engineering. If the analytical question is whether profession-wide reputation can substitute for direct structural separation, this is where to look.

4. **Keep pension/endowment fiduciary regimes as the legal-doctrine reference case.** Trust law's prophylactic enforcement of the duty of loyalty is the most fully developed legal architecture for the structural pattern. Useful for theoretical work on *why* prophylactic enforcement (i.e., punishment for conflict-of-interest transactions even absent demonstrable harm) might be the right enforcement design.

5. **Do not include P&T committees, internal capital allocation, or REF panels as positive examples.** Each case has serious empirical or structural problems with one of the requirements. They are valuable as *contrast* cases — illustrations of failure modes — but presenting them as stabilisation cases would misread the literature.

**Thresholds that would change these recommendations.** (a) If new empirical work showed that conglomerate winner-picking is actually robustly value-creating in firms with focused strategies (Stein 1997's prediction), internal capital allocation should be promoted from ambiguous to good fit. (b) If the OSFI elimination of mandatory actuarial peer review in 2027 produced no degradation in reserving quality over a five-year window, this would weaken the case that peer review (rather than ASOPs alone) is what stabilises actuarial honesty. (c) If a future REF cycle were redesigned with cross-pot blind review (as PLOS Biology 2025 distributed peer review proposals contemplate), the REF could move from ambiguous to strong fit.

## Caveats

1. **The "stabilisation" question is partly definitional.** All five strong/good cases show *some* documented gaming or bias under stress. The question is whether bias is bounded relative to the discretion available, not whether it is zero. I have applied a relative rather than absolute standard.

2. **Selection effects in the empirical record.** Cases that fail spectacularly (Enron-era audit committees, FAA-Boeing 737 MAX organization-designation authority) generate large literatures; cases that succeed quietly produce regulatory-routine literature. Survivor bias likely makes the strong cases look better than they are and the failure cases look worse. The Ashenfelter exchangeability tests are the main exception — they directly test the null of bias.

3. **Direction of gaming matters more than the question may imply.** In SSCs, fishery scientists' professional incentives could push toward *more conservative* ABCs (precautionary approach is professionally rewarded) — this is the opposite direction from EU Council ministers' incentives. The "stabilisation" we observe in U.S. fisheries is partly the result of giving binding authority to a group whose biases run *against* the gaming direction the political body would otherwise indulge. This complicates simple "stabilisation despite self-interest" narratives.

4. **Theoretical formalisation is uneven.** Only final-offer arbitration has a worked-out formal model of the stabilisation mechanism. SSC, actuarial, and fiduciary cases are largely treated descriptively in their respective professional and legal literatures, without a unifying mechanism design treatment. This is itself a research gap rather than an evaluation of the cases.

5. **Several primary sources cited above are hosted by professional or regulatory bodies with their own institutional interests** (CAS Actuarial Review, OSFI guidelines, NOAA reports, NIH CSR materials). Where their claims of stabilisation are institutionally self-interested, I have noted independent academic literature (Ashenfelter, Stein, Friesen et al., Pier et al., Methot et al.) as corroboration or correction.

6. **The April 2026 NIH funding turbulence** (Trump-administration grant cancellations, indirect-cost cap proposals, "forward funding" structural changes) materially reshapes the NIH peer-review case. C&EN reporting from March 2026 documents real politicisation pressure that did not exist when the established peer-review-stabilisation literature (Pier et al. 2018; CIHR 2017) was written. Any current analysis of NIH study sections must treat them as an institution under stress, not in steady state.
